---
title: "Free and cheap backcountry camping in Michigan"
date: 2026-07-08
---

One of Michigan's biggest advantages over a lot of other states: dispersed camping on state forest land is legal, free, and genuinely plentiful — you just have to know the rules.

## State forest dispersed camping (free)

Michigan's state forest system covers roughly 4 million acres, mostly across the northern Lower Peninsula and Upper Peninsula. Unlike state parks, dispersed camping on state forest land is generally allowed without a fee or reservation, as long as you:

- Camp at least a set distance from roads, trails, and water sources (check current DNR guidance for exact distances, since these are enforced)
- Practice Leave No Trace — no established sites means no infrastructure, so trash, waste, and fire management is entirely on you
- Stay within posted state forest boundaries (not adjacent private land, which is common in checkerboard-pattern forest ownership in Michigan)

This is different from state park backcountry camping, which usually requires a designated site and a permit fee.

## State park backcountry camping (low-cost)

If you're hiking a state park backcountry route — Porcupine Mountains, for example — you'll need a Recreation Passport for vehicle entry plus a separate backcountry camping fee, typically in the $15-20/night range depending on the park. This isn't free, but it's still inexpensive compared to many Western park systems.

## National Forest land (free, with caveats)

Michigan has two National Forests — the Huron-Manistee and the Ottawa/Hiawatha in the U.P. — where dispersed camping is generally free under similar rules to state forest land. National Forest roads can be rougher and less marked than state forest access points, so this suits people comfortable navigating without cell service.

## What's not free

- Isle Royale requires a paid park entrance fee even though backcountry camping itself is free — see the [Isle Royale budget guide](/guides/isle-royale-on-a-budget/)
- Michigan State Parks with designated campgrounds (not backcountry) charge standard nightly fees, similar to car camping anywhere else
- Some county and metropark systems require their own separate pass, not covered by the Recreation Passport

## The practical takeaway

If your trip stays entirely on state forest or National Forest land, your camping cost can genuinely be $0. The moment your route crosses into a state park backcountry zone or a national park, budget for a modest per-night fee — but even then, Michigan stays cheap relative to most other states.
